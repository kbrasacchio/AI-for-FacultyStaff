A Brief History

Imagined First. Built Second. Until Now.

Fiction imagined it. Science built it. For 3,000 years they chased each other — until now, when they're almost indistinguishable.

🎭 Fiction Imagined It
⚙️ Reality Built It
~800 BCE — Ancient Greece
Gods & Their Machines
Homer's Iliad describes Hephaestus crafting golden handmaidens that could move and speak. Talos, a bronze giant, guarded Crete. Artificial life as divine craft — imagination running centuries ahead of the tools to build it.
~800–600 BCE — Greece & Egypt
Temple Automata
Greek and Egyptian temples housed mechanized cult statues — figures that could move their arms, pour libations, and "speak" through hidden priest-operated mechanisms. Worshippers believed the gods inhabited them. Engineering in service of awe.
~250 BCE — Greek Myth
Pygmalion & Daedalus
Pygmalion sculpts a woman so perfect he falls in love with her — Aphrodite brings her to life. Daedalus, the legendary craftsman, was said to build statues that moved and needed to be chained down. You know Pygmalion: it became My Fair Lady.
~250 BCE — Alexandria
The First Real Automata
Engineers built hydraulic singing birds, steam-powered moving statues, and mechanical figures. Hero of Alexandria wrote On Making Automata. For the first time, the dream wasn't myth — it was blueprints.
1818 — Mary Shelley
Frankenstein
The first science fiction novel asks: What do we owe what we create? Written by an 18-year-old during the Industrial Revolution. The ethics question, 200 years early.
1822 — Charles Babbage
The Difference Engine
The first mechanical computer — a machine that could calculate. Pure mathematics, no "intelligence" yet. But the idea of a thinking machine had crossed from fiction into science.
1920 — Karel Čapek
R.U.R. — The Word "Robot" Is Born
Czech playwright coins "robot" (from forced labor). Robots created to serve revolt against their creators. The cultural script: create → fear → consequence.
1950 — Alan Turing
Can Machines Think?
Turing's landmark paper proposes the "Imitation Game" — if a machine can converse indistinguishably from a human, does it matter whether it's "really" thinking? Still debated today.
1942 — Isaac Asimov
The Three Laws of Robotics
Asimov rejects the revolt narrative and proposes a framework: robots must not harm humans, must obey orders, must protect themselves — in that order. AI as a design problem, not a horror premise.
1956 — Dartmouth Conference
AI Is Officially Born
John McCarthy coins the term "artificial intelligence." A group of scientists formally launch the field. Asimov is writing his laws. Fiction and science are now in full conversation.
1968 — Stanley Kubrick
HAL 9000
2001: A Space Odyssey gives us an AI that is calm, helpful, and then quietly homicidal when its mission conflicts with its crew. The AI alignment problem — in cinematic form.
1997 — IBM Deep Blue
Machine Beats Champion
Deep Blue defeats world chess champion Garry Kasparov. A machine outperforms the best human mind at the game considered the pinnacle of human intellect. The world takes notice.
2013 — Spike Jonze
Her
A man falls in love with his AI operating system. The film imagines an AI that is emotionally present, deeply intelligent, and ultimately beyond human understanding. Released 9 years before ChatGPT.
2017 — Google Research
"Attention Is All You Need"
A landmark paper introduces the Transformer architecture — the engine behind every modern large language model. The engineering finally catches up to the dream.
November 2022 — ChatGPT 100 million users in 2 months. Faster than any technology in history.

A Bigger Frame

We are witnessing evolution.

And most of us don't realize it — because we've been taught to think of evolution as something else entirely.

How We Usually Think of Evolution

When most people hear "evolution," they picture: apes becoming humans. Dinosaurs going extinct. Darwin on the Galápagos. The slow grind of natural selection across millions of years.

It's a biological story — change through random mutation and survival pressure, measured in geological time. Gradual. Unconscious. Happening to us, not by us.

What's Actually Happening Now

Technological evolution has always been thought of as "advancement" — linear, progressive, tools getting better. The wheel → the engine → the computer. Improvement, but not transformation.

AI is something different. It's not just a better tool — it's a tool that learns, adapts, and generalizes. That is the definition of evolution.

In 1863 — before Darwin had even fully published his theory — Samuel Butler wrote a paper called Darwin Among the Machines, asking whether machines might one day evolve consciousness and surpass us as the dominant species.

Samuel Butler, 1863 — 160 years before ChatGPT

🦕

Biological Evolution

Random mutation. Millions of years. Survival pressure. We study it in fossils.

⚙️

Technological Advancement

Intentional. Incremental. Better tools. We study it in museums.

🧠

AI Evolution

Systems that learn and adapt. Measured in months. We're watching it right now.

What this means for educators

Every generation of teachers has had to help students navigate a world in transition. The printing press. Industrialization. The internet. Each time, the tools changed — but the mission didn't.

The difference this time: the pace of change has accelerated past what any single generation has experienced before. You are not watching history unfold from a distance. You are in the middle of it. That's not a burden — it's an extraordinary place to be.

What makes it powerful

  • → Trained on a huge portion of human writing — books, articles, code, conversations
  • → Billions of parameters (weights) encode patterns about language and knowledge
  • → Can generalize — applies learned patterns to new situations it hasn't seen

What makes it limited

  • ! No memory between conversations (unless given one)
  • ! Knowledge has a cutoff date — it doesn't know what happened last week
  • ! "Hallucinations" — it can generate confident-sounding falsehoods

Prompt Engineering

The quality of what you get out of AI is almost entirely determined by what you put in. Good prompting is a skill — and it's learnable in about 10 minutes.

The R·T·C·F Framework

R
Role
Give the AI a persona or expertise level to speak from.
"Act as an experienced PK–12 educator…"
T
Task
Be specific about exactly what you want it to do.
"Write a parent email explaining our new AI policy…"
C
Context
Provide the background, audience, and any constraints.
"…for families of 6th–8th graders, tone: warm and direct…"
F
Format
Tell it how you want the output structured.
"…in 3 short paragraphs, no jargon, under 200 words."

Weak vs. Strong Prompts

❌ Weak prompt Write a lesson plan about the Civil War.
✅ Strong prompt Act as a middle school history teacher. Create a 45-minute lesson plan on the causes of the Civil War for 7th graders with mixed reading levels. Include a warm-up activity, two discussion questions, and an exit ticket. Use simple language.
❌ Weak prompt Give me feedback on this student essay.
✅ Strong prompt Act as an 8th grade English teacher. Review the essay below and give specific, constructive feedback on thesis clarity, use of evidence, and transitions. Limit feedback to 3–4 actionable bullet points. Do not rewrite the essay. [paste essay]

Policies & Frameworks

SAES has done the work to create clear expectations for both faculty and students. Here's what you need to know — and how to use the frameworks in your classroom.

The Three-Level Framework for Student Work

Use this system to set clear expectations for each assignment. Be explicit — students need to know the level for every piece of work.

1
No Use
Prohibit all AI tools. Use when assessing individual knowledge or skills that must be demonstrated independently.
2
Conditional Use
Permitted only with prior approval for specific purposes. Requires the acknowledgment form. Good for editing, science/math writing clarity.
3
Unconditional Use
AI freely allowed. Works well when using AI as a debate partner, brainstorming tool, or research assistant. Students must still cite AI use.
"In this course, there will be opportunities to responsibly use generative AI to assist your learning. Students must disclose any use of AI-generated material and take full responsibility for checking the accuracy of any output."
— SAES Sample AI Course Policy

Faculty & Student Handbook Details

📘 Faculty Use of AI — Employee Handbook 2025–26 ▼

Faculty may use AI to enhance instructional practices, improve student engagement, streamline workflows, and provide personalized feedback. All use must align with the school's values: integrity, equity, and authentic relationships.

You remain the professional. AI may assist in generating feedback, but faculty are fully responsible for evaluating student work and assigning grades.

What's off-limits:

  • Sharing student names or PII with AI tools trained on user data
  • Using AI to grade without thorough review
  • Inputting health, counseling, or financial data into public AI systems
  • Relying on AI content without verifying accuracy and alignment with school goals

Deeper Issues

AI is powerful, useful — and genuinely complicated. These are the questions worth sitting with as educators.

AI learns from human-generated data — including human biases.

If the training data over-represents certain voices, perspectives, or cultures, the AI reflects that. Historical bias in text becomes encoded bias in outputs.

Studies have shown AI tools producing lower-quality results for names associated with certain racial or ethnic groups, and generating stereotyped descriptions in image generation.

For educators: Teach students to interrogate AI outputs. Ask: Whose voice is this? What perspective is missing?

Try It Yourself

Ask an AI to "describe a doctor." Then ask it to "describe a nurse." Notice patterns in the defaults.

Ask it to write a history of [any topic]. Ask: What's left out? Who's not centered?

AI is a tool. Humans are responsible for the outcomes.

When you submit a document written with AI assistance, your name is on it. When you use AI to draft feedback for a student, you are responsible for that feedback.

AI can hallucinate — it invents citations, misquotes research, and states falsehoods with confidence. Always verify.

The faculty handbook is clear: AI is a support tool, not a substitute for professional judgment.

Practical Verification Habit

Every time AI gives you a specific fact, statistic, or citation — check it. Paste it into Google. It takes 15 seconds and has saved countless professionals from embarrassment.

Treat AI output like a first draft from a very fast intern: often useful, never complete, always your responsibility.

AI doesn't know St. Andrew's. You do.

Advancement work, teaching, and pastoral care run on genuine human relationships. AI can help draft — it cannot replace the voice, the history, or the relationship.

The risk isn't just plagiarism. It's the slow erosion of authentic engagement — when we stop writing in our own voice because it's faster to let AI do it.

The discipline: Use AI to get to a first draft faster. Then make it yours.

Authenticity Matters

AI can draft. It can organize. It can get you 90% of the way to something usable. But it doesn't know your students, your culture, your gut sense of what's true. That knowledge is irreplaceable — and it's what transforms a competent draft into something worth sending.

Access to AI is not evenly distributed.

The most powerful AI tools cost money. Students and families with more resources can access better tools. This creates a new vector for inequity in education.

SAES's 1:1 program and Google Workspace access help level this within our community. But it's a real tension to hold — and to name with students.

Also consider: AI performs differently across languages, dialects, and cultural contexts. English-dominant AI tools disadvantage multilingual learners.

Discussion Starter

With students: "If AI makes writing easier, does it make writing matter less? Or does it change what writing is for?"

This is not a rhetorical question. Sit with it.
